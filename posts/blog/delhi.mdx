## On Partition and nationalism

I also discovered that Faqir Chand was established in Peshawar in 1931, and moved to Delhi in 1951. So much of Delhi, and I suppose North India, lives in the shadow cast by the partition. I didn’t understand it until I spent time here - every single person, place, and situation has a partition story to them. People whose ~~ancestors~~ grandparents came over, areas whose cultures were shaped by the villages they were populated from 400km away, wealth to be inferred from the caste of the refugees allotted land therein, monuments that housed camps of displaced people for years.

I have been fascinated with Delhi the way Mahit was taken by [Teixcalaan](https://www.goodreads.com/series/233352-teixcalaan) - an imperial power that was not mine, but associated with me everywhere I went. I am unable to think about nationalism without understanding it as imperialism - but it is still a nationalism I was brought up in, a patriotism I was brought up to cling to [as part of my identity](https://tanvibhakta.in/poetry/day-8/) - to hold on to a home I didn’t know growing up far away surrounded by strangeness my parents couldn’t, wouldn’t, understand. Delhi represents this seat of power that has nothing to do with me, yet everything to do with what I am, and defines the limits of what I can become, what I can exist as. I reject the imperial north in its great generalization of reducing food to tomato-onion based curries, and reducing weddings to elaborate affairs with brides decked in red lehengas, at the same time as I am jealous of its poetry, its unifying power, its rich cultural history, and its representation in media that I couldn’t consume because it was so alien and yet a diet I assimilated to via simple proximity.

I hopped over from Khan Market to Mandi house, finding myself with a full hour to spare before a play I had tickets for, so I walked around and found myself in the [Lalit Kala Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lalit_Kala_Akademi), which was exhibiting 65 works by 65 artists for its 65th anniversary. At first, I was a little miffed - these were individual pieces that seemed to have no rhyme or reason to be put together, or placed next to each other. There seemed to be no curatory thread tying this work together. But that allowed me a range of freedom I had not experienced until that moment. I was able to look at each (formidable) piece on its own merits, moving on when it didn’t interest me, and being captivated when it suited me, without having to hold in my head the larger narrative being told. I regret only being able to spend an hour there, and not being able to go back later to finish the gallery.

The play I had tickets for is called [Ilhaam](https://www.mumbaitheatreguide.com/dramas/hindi/ilaham.asp). It contained so many pretty words (in hindi) I didn’t understand, performed by people who were supposed to be from the best theatre school in the country (National School of Drama, but this might be contested) - though whether that is due to talent, or mere proximity to power, is something I don’t understand (what’s the difference?). It is a fascinating story that illustrates how you cannot achieve enlightenment without being unintelligible to the people you care for the most. Or is it about how some mental illness comes on suddenly and makes you incomprehensible - and perhaps being in the state of “illness” feels so right, you might not want to leave? ## Characteristics of dilli chaat

All golgappe in Delhi seem to be made from sooji ie rava, not plain wheat. This adds to it a crunch and richer texture that is missing from what I typically eat back home. I didn’t even know this was a thing! But I will say that I felt the aloo filling was lacking something (though I didn’t mention this at the time any of my compatriots for fear of crucifixion). The filling is too lightly spiced, and there is too little of it in the puri. The puri itself is just slightly too large for comfort. The whole golguppa is primarily a vessel for, and relies on the spicing of, the two spicy and sweet paanis (no chutney here). There are also only 5 to a plate. I would much rather have a slightly smaller poori - it fits in my mouth better, and have six or eight to a plate making it easy to share with a friend. But the sooji also makes for a thicker shell, which transports the paani more safely.

![](images/IMG_20260824_165731640.webp "This kebab was bits of soya, paneer, mushroom, capsicum, skewered and charred in a tandoor, and then cooked in a gravy of tomato plus fats and spices I couldn’t make out")

A bhalla is a ball of urad and moong daal batter. Fried, then dipped in water until it becomes a sponge. Drained, and drowned in curd + spices. Not a big fan - hot+crunchy+cold curd vada is definitely better. We wanted to take away the bhalla after eating just a spoon; but when we asked the man to reseal it, instead of telling us it was against policy or whatever, the staff got bug eyed and raised his voice, saying he wouldn’t be touching the bowl again. This reaction is such a blatant example of casteism that even someone from an upper caste and visibly upper class person faces. In [another instance](/#trolled), a man who struck up conversation with us at a table we were sharing (itself an anomaly) offered us some of the food he had ordered. When we politely declined, he said, “Well I also have to show na, that I’m liberal and all?”, and proceeded to polish off his plate without asking again.

I felt othered in my eating habits much more in Delhi than I have elsewhere. I am handed spoons and katoris by default - when I say I don’t want them, I am looked at askew. I have travelled lots and been in all kinds of mixed company. I am not afraid to use my hands. But in Delhi, I felt a little exotic, to insist on eating rice with my hands and having the liquids touch means denying the tools of polite civilisation.

## Matryoshka doll city

The culture of a city is keenly defined by the generations of the people who live here. Delhi is still affected and influenced by the tragedy of the partition (from eighty years ago) because people from that time are still here, even if they are 4 generations removed. To exist then, in Delhi, is to have a history of your own that spans a hundred years in one location, with the continuity of personal stories, place and placement, business partnerships, and community relationships - that nonetheless can’t hold a candle to the parks, thoroughways, and everyday monuments that were built 600 years prior. It is impossible, as an outsider, to live in Delhi and have a moment of real vulnerability with anyone who has lived here for three generations- unless you come pre-vetted by an introduction, or by virtue of your wealth, class, caste, education, location markers (which all act as a proxy for proof) to establish a world of shared understanding of power and trauma. Without this, there is simply too much to lose. Or, you can have this moment of emotional vulnerability with people who choose to leave the city and find you themselves.

I wanted to add some woven fabric to my wardrobe, and Delhi is very good for those fabrics, so I thought I might try my hand at a lesser market (not having the time to travel or the patience to brave the crowds in the greater). We made our way to Kamla Nagar market, where we saw

1. A clock tower
2. A fresh farsan place that smelled very good
3. Several clothes I didn’t like and one set that was too amusing to be left behind
4. A man selling achaar from a cart
5. An asafoetida factory
6. A lane exclusively selling glassware for chemistry

## Expected Delhi Violence

Having tired ourselves out on boxy fits that didn’t, we made our way back homewards for the main event of the trip - locating the places all the major events in Ankur’s stories had taken place. Over the course of the evening I saw the bus stops he and his friends had regularly met to drink at, the thelas where they had gone to smoke up, the cinemas they had frequented (and the terraces they had snuck up on to parkour off afterwards). We didn’t go to his college because we would have had to burn it down and we didn’t have time to deal with an arson case. We parked on the backside of an M2K that seemed to be entirely populated with cellphone stores. We ate a surprisingly good bun maska chai, skipped the Bittoo Tikki Wala because of the loss of quality following its franchisee status, and ordered a large amount of chaap from Jaiveer Naan and Chaap, including a plate to take back and freeze for my mom, who had never eaten it before. On the way back (to the wall we had parked against, which stunk of piss even though there was a public toilet 200m away - we saw with our own eyes a man walk out and do the deed where we were going to park so we had to park slightly away) I saw an actual Delhiism! There was a large crowd gathered around silently, watching a very noisy two or three people go at it. There was already a policeman involved, trying to make sense of what was happening. The drama! I desperately wanted to know what was happening, but I was the only woman I saw in the area so I didn’t feel like I could ask in my broken Hindi. But Ankur took initiative and asked someone who filled him in eagerly and with no compunctions - apparently someone had thrown a brick at the person currently talking to the police and run away. Our man was bleeding from the forehead! And his friends were encouraging him to not run away, and stay and talk instead. Considering that our man was already in need of stitches I don’t think he should care about being a snitch. When we finally pulled out of the parking lot, the police van containing him and his friends sped out ahead of us.

I was so tired and in pain by the time I got home. I took advantage of my host family’s excellent in-home gym, and stretched a little bit before eating. Dinner was chaap, some excellent home chicken, roti of the ragi and regular kind, and some more of the paan we had picked up the previous night. Then we sat down to do deliberations - we were scheduled to do the [Taj Mahal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taj_Mahal) the next day but some constraints came up last minute (such as friends not communicating some apprehensions _ahem_) so we pushed the Agra trip to Wednesday, and decided to go to Mehrauli instead. Having freed ourselves from the responsibility of an early morning, we promptly stayed up until 2am playing video games and chatting.

This meant that I woke up at 10am the next morning, knowing I didn’t have to work (how excellent to have no responsibilities!) so I really lazed around. There was chai, and an hour later, breakfast, and two hours after that, lunch was scheduled. I went for a solitary walk around the area to look at the DDA market and the parks (and see if I could purchase a glass nail file - I could not). I still had the time and was feeling the lack of movement, so then I ran on the treadmill, and that confused me lots. My Garmin said I ran at the truly snail’s pace of 13min/km (ig this is a good thing, because I’ve been trying to slow down) but the treadmill said I was doing 9min/km, which is what I was aiming for. I am choosing to not count either - I just know that I ran for about 45min, listening to an intense textbook the whole time because I didn’t have to keep my own pace. I appreciated that - hell, I do have moderately reliable access to a treadmill close to home as well now. Might make this a regular thing.

## Unexpected Delhi Violence

Just as we were putting on our shoes and getting ready to go, we got a panicked call. Aunty and Uncle had gotten into an accident, and could we please call 112 and get the cops? Their line wasn’t going through. What?? Ankur and Ankush left to the site of the collision post-haste, but I stayed back for about an hour, then lugged a tokri full of chchaas and lunch items to where they were so that uncle could take his meds. We all learnt about the importance of backseat airbags that day. I also saw a Haryanvi accent, and the motion of the wheel of law from the side of power. The formalities took a long time and involved many phone calls. A bus had jumped a red light and at full speed hurtled towards and T-boned the vehicle aunty and uncle were in. There was no doubt as to whose fault it was, and to the seriousness of the accident. Uncle said several times that if they had been in a smaller car, they would have been paste on the side of the road. As it was, from within the car they both sustained several large bruises and small wounds. The problem here was that the accident had taken place in the center of an intersection - the same intersection that marked the borders of two different precincts, and the cops could not agree on whose jurisdiction this would be handled.

The sun was setting by the time we were heading home. I wanted to head into the city, but nothing on my menu seemed interesting enough (or looked like I could make it there in time) so I just got off to eat kachori chaat and paneer pakoda at a sweet shop (which is the equivalent of a darshini there) and then walked back home.

## Ghar

Home, in my host family, is a very lively affair. First of all, there are the dogs - Molly, and Kia. They are a Maltese and a Teacup Maltese respectively - yes, the teacup Maltese weighs less than a kilogram! All I want is to create a slightly larger version of this green thing from IKEA and have her lounge in it for photoshoots. They are soft and fluffy and absolutely need to smell everything you bring into the house, yes, right now, immediately. They confirm my theory that cats are just dogs, because they _also_ follow their human around room to room, sitting quietly under some piece of furniture or lounging on a sofa nearby while the humans talk. I spent the first four days in this household battling the desire to pull them into my lap and have them sit while I did anything - and the last three days giving in.

Next there are Bittoo and Sanju. Bittoo helps with feeding the family, the guests, the people related to various businesses that come and go, the dogs, the birds, and himself. Sanju manages the travel, the cars, and the important odd jobs that the family might want done across the two businesses. Then there are Ankur’s family themselves. This home is one that contains a great love for each other (and puns). I can see why Ankur tells the best stories - his mother is very good at them- but if you’re in the family you have no choice but to polish this skill. Entertainment is the name of the game in casual conversation. Over the course of various mealtime conversations, I learnt about how various sets of grandparents crossed over the border. I learnt about aunty’s childhood food. I developed an independent relationship with aunty entirely seeded from mealtime conversations, where I was honored to be vulnerable with her. Ankur’s family really took me in and made me feel at home, in a way that I haven’t felt in any other family, including my own.

My own family is afraid of vulnerability. We walk on eggshells made from the years of trauma we have caused each other. When we make fun of each other, it turns mean, and becomes bullying very fast. But here, even if someone was being made fun of, they could shrug it off. Infuriating idiosyncrasies are indulgently laughed at and accepted, instead of being used as ammunition to dismiss the person who has them. I understand that no family is perfect, but their day to day was so much more fun than I know mine typically is, in my childhood home. It would be easy to be jealous, but instead I was sad. What would it take to have this for myself, for us?

I can see that Ankur and Ankush have put in significant effort to build a relationship with each other, and then their parents as adults. I know that this started when they were both much older, and that’s a line my brother hasn’t crossed yet - he is still a child, even at 22. But despite the childhood full of differences and fights they had, they have a core of shared values to build on top of. They both want to grow, and become better human beings, and have a shared understanding of what that “better” looks like. In a family where displays of affection and emotion are commonplace, they are able to access it for each other taking its presence at face value. They don’t have to make it something it is not. They’re able to see each other as people, not as siblings. I am, perhaps, jealous of that. My family can and will come around to the lifestyle I choose to live. To a certain extent, I have that dinner table conversation with my parents now when they come over - even if they seem to have taken a vow to not eat at my house - we’re able to sit around, gossip, plan logistics, hear each other out, laugh. But I cannot get over how different my brother’s value systems are from mine, the way I can with my parents. I expect better from him - more empathy from him, more understanding, better behavior. I wonder if this makes me a worse human being - it’s only useful to expect better from people when they have demonstrated their desire to grow. Why do I insist on foisting my own expectations of who he should be onto him? I know that regardless of what I should or shouldn’t be feeling about him, who he is makes it very hard to have a relationship with him.

After yet another excellent dinner (where we were all very grateful to various divine beings, and also to the backseat airbags), we did more logistics. Hiccups that I should have seen came up... and so we made the sad decision to put off Agra for the next trip. GG, who had recommended the excellent city walk, had also recommended an Agra tour guide who typically accompanies prime ministers and is intimately familiar with the histories. So we were very sad to not experience this. I don’t generally do the touristy thing when I travel (almost like it’s a challenge. Almost like I’m afraid to be cringe.) but I knew that I wanted to go see the Taj Mahal the moment I planned to go to Delhi. I wanted to drive a car fast down a highway. I wanted to wear a blue dress and take gorgeous 9am pictures in the backdrop. I wanted to see what it was really that [Shah Jahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah_Jahan) had to say in his monument to love. Well, it wasn’t to be.

Wednesday morning we left for Mehrauli without major incident (except that of being late, which was now an accepted theme of this trip). The [qutb complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutb_Minar_complex) was far enough away that it was wise to break our journey up and grab lunch, so we went to [LHA kitchen](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Lha+Kitchen+restaurant+Delhi), Ankush’s favorite restaurant and the place with the third best pork momos I have eaten (and the best in India). We also ate a spicy pork stir fry, charred beans in chilli oil, chicken mother momos, butter tea, some absolutely fantastic ema datshi with tingmo, after which I did some cursory shopping continuing my search for a cotton bush shirt. When I went to Thailand a few years ago, I went with a budget and the desire to update my wardrobe. The next time I feel the need to overhaul my wardrobe, I will be going to Delhi instead. There are a lot of Uniqlo-style pieces available for half the price around Majnu Ka Tilla and similar areas.

We finally made it to the Qutb complex around 4pm. It is not very large, and I appreciated the little context we had from Rameen’s walk earlier. Unfortunately, because I didn’t have context otherwise for the place, I wasn’t able to appreciate it the way I could have. I did feel good about finding some nice frames for photography. We also saw an interesting plaque in marble that had some parts redacted - by literally cutting the marble out. Now I need to find out what the story there was. ## Ghoomie

I really enjoy driving around in Delhi in a way that I didn’t ever expect to, and in a way that doesn’t make sense for any other city. The roads are wide, and even if there is traffic it is always moving. It takes time to travel because the distances are long, not because the infrastructure is broken - and anyway, if you’re on the road, it is because you chose to take a car for some reason and not the metro instead. When you choose something, it becomes more your responsibility, and you figure out how to make that experience fun for you. Cars in Delhi reflect this - the people are on the roads not because they have to be for transit, but because they want to be to save that 20 minutes, or they want to let people know that they have the money for a car and the ability to command someone to drive them around in it - in which case, bigger is better. Cars also own the road in a way that I haven’t experienced in other cities. Delhi with its metro and wide footpaths and parks everywhere is walkable in a way that makes things easy - I walked about 7km (or 7k steps) every day - but find a place that doesn’t have a footpath and you’re in trouble. I’ve had more close calls with cars passing within an inch of my person at 30kmph in my ten days in Delhi than I have had in several years of insisting on walking through Bangalore. This drive back, with the three of us, excellent music, banter, and flying through Delhi at dusk is a fond memory. I also understand my friends who go “ghoomie!!” now.

Once again we did dinner at home. This time, Aunty had sourced for me some [colostrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colostrum#Bovine_colostrum)! Aunty and Uncle also run a gaushala on the outskirts of Delhi. Colostrum is the very fatty milk that is given by the cow in the first two weeks of a calf being born. In my side of town we make an excellent jiggly sweet from this milk called [poss](https://www.rakshaskitchen.com/colostrum-pudding-poss-kharwas-colostrum-recipe/). Unfortunately I didn't have the time (or the confidence) to spend time in the kitchen to make this dish for aunty :( Apparently end-of-August is one of two birthing seasons for cows. I have relatives who reared cows and seen them being brought up in passing; but not at the scale at which this farm does it. Uncle is also particular about the species of the cows, the bulls, and the process. The cows here are treated and fed so well! I regret not being able to visit the farm despite being in Delhi for ten days. Something that has to be saved for next time, just like the Taj Mahal? Aunty was kind enough to give me some ghee to take back home. It is my special-occasion ghee now.

Dinners were always embellished with this excellent produce - curd set with a thick fatty layer of curd-cream on top at the end of every meal. White butter made from this milk fresh with every paratha. The ghee, of course, on the phulkas, rotis, breads of various kinds. The paneer.... fresh, soft, light and soaks up flavour. Goes excellently in a paneer paratha. And the tea, of course, served all day long with the thick milk. Dinners were always a lively affair, populated by such excellent dishes that I regularly bemoaned how small my appetite had become over the years. Another night, we had some mutton where the tendon fell apart from the bone just as I was done with it, so I could suck up the marrow! Uncle informed me that this is the mark of a skilled butcher.

I stayed up this night finishing [Those Pricey Thakur Girls](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/17340720-those-pricey-thakur-girls), a book by [Anuja Chauhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anuja_Chauhan) (of [The Zoya Factor](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/3840329-the-zoya-factor) fame, which I actually read when I was in school) that follows the exploits of 5 sisters who grow up in old Delhi and their marriage market potential. It has a slightly rang-de-basanti vibe. Mid-trip was the perfect time to read it. It contains lots of Delhi-isms - I have a whole note to publish about reading it. Unexpectedly, the book has a way to relate to home via the Mangalore Christian protagonist.

## The Qawwali that didn’t

I woke up Thursday knowing it was back to work for me. When I was planning this Delhi trip from the comfort of home in Bangalore, I thought I would just go to cafes in various localities depending on what that evening’s plans were and work out of there, but that didn’t pan out - I was too comfortable in Ankur’s home, I was waking up tired and late, and I was finding that the food experience in Delhi was very unreliable - if it didn’t come vetted, it was most likely to be a miss. I finished work that evening later than I would have liked, and we ended up cabbing it to the [Nizamuddin Dargah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizamuddin_Dargah) for [qawwali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qawwali) night. I had heard that they do qawwali every Thursday night, and that they start at 7pm. I didn’t know what to expect, so I assumed it would be a scene like Ranga Shankara where the doors might close after the allocated time - or there might be a crowd too large for us to safely or comfortably experience this. I should have known that this is India. Places of worship have no notion of crowd control, and the ghettoization of Muslim and muslim-adjacent areas is in full swing. So when we showed up, there was only the generic (very heavy) crowd and no signs or scenes for a performance. Within twenty minutes of our confused looking about, people started sitting down, so we sat too! And we waited. And waited. There was a crying baby I distracted with flying silver foil crinkles. A rail of a teenage boy wearing a brown skull cap sat down in the middle of our congregation facing the tomb and started a solo devotional singing session. We spotted a pin of a green dino that said _cuntasaurous!_. We chatted with a young chap doing a solo bike trip through north India from Kerala. And we waited. Finally, at some point a rotund boy not older than 10 came and rose us.  
\<voice note of me doing an imitation of utoh Utah aaa such mai hone Wala main! Wo nakhli qawali baitha hain, utoh!>

We were mildly outraged on behalf of the teenager, who had been singing in folk tunes for an hour and a half by then, but we were otherwise disappointed and went on our way. Because the previous day was [Eid-al-milad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mawlid) and they had done a qawali, and they would do a qawwali for Friday, they had cancelled the qawwali ordinarily scheduled for this day. So we got up, looked at the tomb of Humayun's daughter to the side, got yelled at for attempting to go inside the dargah’s smallest room (why? We had our heads covered!), and checked out the mosque attached to the complex before we left.

The state of the mosque made me very sad. The men’s room (I peeped in) had an air of sacredness and people in active prayer. The women’s room (off to the side) had literal garbage piled at the entrance spilling over - the opposite of a sacred environment.

## Acquiring an education

We walked to the metro and passed some clearly homeless people who were nonetheless conducting a very involved conversation about the time and reign of [Indira Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Gandhi). It shocked Ankur. How can you be “educated” and still be on the streets? But so much about how Delhi runs is about who you know and how you know them. And so much of housing, employment, access, 80 years of independence later, still relies on who you know. In Bangalore, when I decided to distance myself from family, I was still able to waltz into the goodwill of various friends on my own merit - and because many people are immigrants, and therefore households are made up of peers, you can even crash on a couch for 6 months or so until you figure things out. Is that possible to do somewhere as reliant on family connections as Delhi?

There is also the conflating of “an education” with the understanding of current affairs. It’s a mistake I see a lot of my upper-class, well-to-do friends make. Participating in a formalised learning system is not an exclusive marker for intelligence.

## The worth of a woman

We made our way to Khan Market’s [Tranzzit bar](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Tranzzit+bar+Khan+Market+Delhi) to hang out with Avani, her colleague, and Ankush (we just missed his girlfriend!) and attempt to salvage the day. Walking in, I could feel the city try to beat me into the shape of how its women should be. I felt so unprepared walking in sweaty, with no makeup, wearing pants that tapered down _towards_ my ankle, instead of _away_. Traveling while being gender non-conforming is scary (I don’t have too much experience doing it) so I had decided I would be femme. But femme in Delhi is very different from femme in Bangalore. Femme in Delhi is a full face of makeup, an A-line dress and heels, and a performance of helplessness, cattiness, and the power of the girl group all at the same time. Thankfully, I’ve had enough experience having to project that I’m a competent successful professional regardless of how I look, so I could ignore the implicit gender expectations and the judgement around my ignorance of them, and attempt to have a good time. We ordered 6 cocktails at the table, and every single one of them was delicious. My second cocktail was a mistake, and I fully passed out in the cab on the way home.

Friday was another day of work, but we did order in some cholle kulcha to eat for/with lunch. I can now confirm that the guy opposite the NIFT campus in Bangalore is legit. I was done with work at about 5pm, and we left for the city again! We got off at Rajiv Chowk and headed to [Jantar Mantar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jantar_Mantar,_New_Delhi). To me, Jantar Mantar isn’t an astronomicall monument. It’s a symbol of resistance in this country. I wanted to feel what it would have been like, to be here when any of the protests were happening. It is such a small place - how can it hold all these people? Does the place ever get so full that people go inside the monuments? Why are they closed off to us anyway? I wanted to understand how the monuments worked and the llms came in clutch here. I pictured what they were explaining to me and finally grokked spatially what I’ve been failing to understand since the 8th grade - how astronomical distances can still make sense as a projection on a flat plane like ours, and the value of trigonometry. I also caught some very pretty frames. Outside the gates, there was a guy selling Ram Laddoo! I had never even heard of this before. What is it made from? I was too full, but Ankur still insisted on eating a small plate and I had to agree it was really good.

## Graffiti: a history of people

The first thing I did after I got over my awe was to look for the scratchmark that says “[Akhil loves Rohit](https://www.vayavya.in/akhil-katyal.html#:~:text=would%20you%20do%20me%20a%20favour)”. I was deeply disappointed to not find it (did a renovation happen after the poem was written?) but there was lots of other graffiti. I love seeing graffiti. I used to be very judgemental of graffiti as a child (it’s what I was taught) but I have now changed my mind. We all want to make our mark on this world. People with resources build monuments, but regular people can’t do that - and so they leave their mark on the world in their own way. Yes, it is a defacement. But it is also an excellent microcosm.

Humayun’s tomb was built by his widow after his death, and hosts at least 150 other Mughal bodies besides. A lot of the Mughal monuments have us thinking about the nature of love. In these places, I also enjoy thinking about other kinds of love - the kind you do with your pasandida aurat, or you have with a best friend at a picnic in 7th grade. Delhi and its monuments are full of little nooks that are made for hideaway couples. Why were they built if they don’t get used?

From Humayun’s tomb, we went to NGMA. What did I like there? The permanent exhibits were decent enough to understand the life and times of the artists showcased, but I wasn’t very impressed. Why was photography banned?
